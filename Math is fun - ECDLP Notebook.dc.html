After the controlled modular exponentiation, the counting register holds every exponent x at once, entangled with ax mod N. Ignore the work register: what remains is supported on an arithmetic progression of spacing r.

|ψ⟩ ∝ Σj |x₀ + jr⟩  ⟶  peaks at y ≈ kQ/r

The inverse QFT does not find the period. It arranges for every y far from a multiple of Q/r to destructively cancel. With Q = 256, r = 4: peaks at y ∈ {0, 64, 128, 192}, and the continued-fraction step stays idle.

On E(𝔽p) the analogue is [x]P and the period is the order of P. Same circuit, different group — the cost moves entirely into the point addition.

Where cancellation actually happens

After the controlled modular exponentiation, the counting register holds every exponent x at once, entangled with ax mod N. Measuring the work register — or simply ignoring it — leaves the counting register in a superposition supported on an arithmetic progression of spacing r.

|ψ⟩ ∝ Σj |x₀ + jr⟩   ⟶QFT⁻¹   peaks at y ≈ kQ/r

The inverse QFT does not find the period. It arranges the phases so that every y that is not close to a multiple of Q/r destructively cancels. What survives is what you measure.

With Q = 2⁸ = 256 and r = 4, the spacing Q/r = 64 is exact, so the peaks are perfectly sharp: y ∈ {0, 64, 128, 192}. Nothing is lost to leakage; the continued-fraction step is trivial here and only earns its keep when r ∤ Q.
